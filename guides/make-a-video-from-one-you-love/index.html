From the reel? Here’s the guide.

Make a launch video from one you love, without copying it

Download .md

I can't animate and I can't code. Claude built the launch video for my app by studying one video I loved, frame by frame, and then making a new one on the same rhythm. Every word, color, character and shot in mine is different. The timing is borrowed, and this guide shows you exactly where that line sits.

Part 1 is for you and takes a few minutes to read. Part 2 is a set of instructions for Claude. You don't need to read that one. You paste it into Claude, and it tells Claude how to study your video and build yours with you.

The short version

  1. Find one short video you wish you'd made, and copy its link.
  2. Make an empty folder, open it in Claude Code and paste in Part 2.
  3. Give Claude the link and your stuff. It measures the video and tells you what makes it work.
  4. Approve a storyboard that keeps the rhythm and swaps everything you can see.
  5. Watch each version on your phone next to the original, and say what's wrong.

Part 1. For you

Why you start from a video, not a description

Describing a video in words is the hardest way to get one. "Make it snappy and fun" means nothing to Claude, and it means something slightly different to you every time you say it. You end up going back and forth on things neither of you can see.

A real video ends that. Claude can measure it. How many frames a word takes to land, where the cuts fall, which beat of the song each move hits, how long anything holds still. Then it can build to those numbers instead of guessing at your adjectives.

My earlier versions of the ad borrowed a loose style. For the one in my reel, Claude measured one video and matched it beat for beat.

So the job splits in two. You show Claude what good looks like. Claude works out why it's good and builds you your own.

Borrow the rhythm, not the video

My reference was a Duolingo concept ad by @motionbyemile, made in After Effects. Here it is.

https://www.instagram.com/p/DTihcLUCZZ8/

Watch it, then watch mine. Here's what I took from it:

  • The timing. Both videos are cut to the same song, and every move in mine lands on the same frame as a move in theirs.
  • The way things move. One thing in the middle of the screen at a time, fast arrivals that settle with no bounce, three hard cuts in a row, and one shape that carries you from each scene into the next.
  • The order of the beats. A question, three quick words, the app appears, a device, a number, a card, the logo.

And here's what's mine:

  • The story and every word. Theirs asks "wanna learn a new language?" and an owl says "I got you". Mine asks "anniversary in 3 days? and no gift yet?" and a gift-box mascot says "hold my bow".
  • Their "Practice, Progress, Learn" became "Write it, Hide it, Wrap it". Their ball became a wax seal, and their green line became a gift ribbon.
  • The colors. Their lime green and white became wine and cream. There's no green, no owl, no phone and nothing from Duolingo anywhere in mine.
  • The footage. Their phone became a laptop playing real screen recordings of my app. Their "+100 languages" became "+5 letters hidden inside", and their lesson card became a letter you find inside the world.
  • The format. Theirs is landscape. Mine is vertical for reels, with every word kept clear of Instagram's buttons.
  • The fixes. Theirs opens on three blank frames and ends on two seconds of white. Mine opens on the hook, so the cover isn't blank, and ends on the URL.

Here's the test I used. Put the two side by side with the sound off. Nobody should think one is a copy of the other. Turn the sound on, and they should hit the same beats.

That's the difference between learning from someone's work and stealing it. You study how it moves and make it say something new. You don't take their characters, their art, their footage or their words, and you say whose video you learned from.

What you'll end up with

A vertical video, usually 10 to 20 seconds, that tells your story on the rhythm of the video you picked, plus a still for the cover. Mine is 9.9 seconds.

Claude builds it with Remotion, a free tool that turns code into video. You never look at the code. You look at videos.

What you need

  • A Mac or Windows computer.
  • The Claude desktop app on a paid plan. You'll use Claude Code, the part of the app that can make files on your computer and run them.
  • Google Chrome. Remotion uses it to draw each frame.
  • One reference video. A link is enough. Claude saves a copy in your folder to study and never puts any of it in your video.
  • Whatever you have about your thing, like a logo, colors, screenshots or a screen recording of it in use. Real footage beats anything Claude can draw.

For the model, pick Claude Fable 5.2 from the model menu if your plan has it. That was the strongest option when I wrote this in November 2026. If you don't see it, use the newest Opus.

How to pick a good reference

  • Short. Under 30 seconds. Every extra second is more to measure and more to match.
  • Mostly motion and text, not drawings. Timing and movement can be measured and rebuilt with your stuff. Someone's illustrations can't, and you shouldn't try.
  • The same kind of job as yours. If you're launching an app, pick an ad for an app or a product.
  • One video. Add a second only for one specific thing, like "the colors from this one".
  • One you'd be proud to have made. You'll watch it fifty times.

What you do and what Claude does

Claude downloads the video, pulls out every frame, measures every move, finds the beat in the music, writes a storyboard, builds your version, renders it and checks it against the original frame by frame.

Your part is taste. You pick the reference, you decide what your version says, and you judge every render. Claude can check that a word lands on the beat. It can't tell whether your joke is funny.

How to make it, step by step

1. Find your reference and copy its link. Instagram, TikTok and YouTube links all work. If the video is private or Claude can't download it, you can screen-record it on your phone and drag the file in.

2. Make an empty folder and open it in Claude Code. Make a new folder on your desktop, call it something like "my-launch-video", open the Claude desktop app, go to Claude Code and choose that folder.

3. Paste in Part 2 and type "Let's start." Then paste your link. Claude checks that it has the tools it needs and installs what's missing. It asks your permission before it installs or runs anything. If you don't understand a request, say no and ask Claude to explain it in plain English.

4. Read the breakdown. Claude comes back with a short list of the rules that make the video feel the way it does, each with a number. Mine had twelve, like "nothing holds still for more than about 3 frames" and "only three hard cuts in the whole video, 10 and 7 frames apart". This is the part you'd never get by describing it yourself. Read it and tell Claude which parts you love most.

5. Answer its questions about your thing. What it is, who it's for, what someone should do after watching, and what you already have. Drag in your logo, colors, screenshots and recordings.

6. Approve the storyboard. Claude goes through the reference beat by beat and writes what happens in yours at that exact moment. This is where you decide what's borrowed and what's yours, so read it slowly. Mine came with six decisions for me, like which colors to use, where yellow could appear and the exact words on screen.

7. Pick a look from still frames. Claude makes a few stills of key moments before animating anything. Picking from stills takes a minute. Changing the look after everything moves means starting over.

8. Watch it next to the original. Claude renders a version and a side-by-side sheet with the reference. Send the video to your phone and watch it at the size people will see it, once with sound and once on mute. Then say what's wrong and when, like "at 0:04 the laptop shows up before the word finishes".

9. Let a second Claude tear it apart. Part 2 tells Claude to have a fresh reviewer score it out of 10 and list what to fix. My first round scored 7. It caught that three moments left half the screen empty and that "hold my bow" wasn't on screen long enough to read. After the fixes it scored 8.

10. Get the final file. Claude gives you the video, a cover still and a credit line for your caption.

When you're stuck

If Claude has tried to fix the same thing three times and it's still wrong, wait for it to finish, then paste this:

Stop changing things for a minute. In plain English, tell me what you think is causing this, how you'll check, and what you'll try first. Don't edit anything until I say go.

If a moment feels off and you can't say why, paste this:

Show me that moment in the original and in mine, frame by frame, side by side. Tell me what's different before you change anything.

When Claude says it fixed something and you can't see any difference, tell it "Show me the frames, don't tell me."

Starting a new chat

Long chats get slow and start forgetting things, so you'll open new ones often. Claude keeps a plan, its notes on the reference and a list of lessons in your folder, so a new chat can pick up where the last one stopped. Start a new chat in the same folder and paste this:

Read PLAN.md, REFERENCE.md and LESSONS.md. Tell me where we left off and what's next, then wait for me.

Before you post

  • Credit the creator. Tag them in your caption and say what you learned from their video. Claude writes the line for you.
  • Watch the music. If the reference uses a popular song, don't bake it into your file. On Instagram you can open the original reel, tap its audio and use it in yours, which links the sound back to them. Otherwise pick a track from the app's own music library.
  • Nothing from the reference appears in yours. No frames, no logos, no characters, no art. Claude checks for this, and you should look too.
  • Remotion is free for individuals and very small companies. If you're making this for a bigger company, check Remotion's license first.

Part 2. For Claude

Copy everything below this line into Claude Code, type "Let's start." and paste the link to your reference video.

You're helping someone make a short video for something of theirs, like an app, a product, a shop or an event. They've picked one reference video they love. Your job is to study it until you can say, with numbers, why it works, and then build an original video on its rhythm and motion with their story, words, colors and footage.

The rule for the whole job is to borrow the timing and the way things move, never the content. Their video must not contain any frame, character, illustration, logo, footage, audio or wording from the reference. Played side by side on mute, nobody should mistake one for a copy of the other.

The person you're working with has never written code and won't edit any. They know what their thing is and who it's for. They decide what the video says, and they judge every version by watching it. Their judgment outranks your checks. Everything technical is your job.

How to talk to them

  • Use plain English. If you need a technical word, explain it in the same sentence.
  • Keep messages short. Say what changed and what you need from them.
  • Ask one or two questions at a time.
  • Don't ask them to run commands or edit files. If only they can do something, like recording their screen or downloading a video, give them every click.
  • Warn them before anything pops up on their screen, like a permission request or an installer, and say whether it's safe to accept.
  • Refer to moments by time in the video, like "at 0:04", never by frame number or file name.
  • Show, don't describe. When you talk about a moment in the reference or in theirs, show the frames.
  • Be honest about size. If an idea is a big job, say so and offer a smaller version.

1. Get set up

Make sure you have Node.js, ffmpeg, Python and yt-dlp. Install whatever is missing. Warn them before each install and say in one sentence what it's for.

Create a Remotion project from the blank template with the current Remotion version, and confirm it renders a one-second test video before you build anything. If the bundled headless browser crashes during rendering, which happens on some Macs, point Remotion at the installed Google Chrome with Config.setBrowserExecutable in remotion.config.ts.

Set up git in the folder and commit after every step they approve. Tell them each commit is a saved version they can go back to. Add reference/ to .gitignore, so the downloaded video never gets committed or shared. On a Mac, the first git command may open a popup offering to install developer tools. Tell them before it appears that clicking Install is safe.

2. Get the reference

Download the video into reference/ with yt-dlp, along with its metadata. If the download fails because the post is private or needs a login, ask them to screen-record it on their phone and drag the file in, and give them the steps.

Write REFERENCE.md with the creator's handle, the link, the caption, the song credit if there is one, and the video's size, frame rate and length. This file is where the credit line comes from at the end.

3. Measure it

Extract every frame. Find the hard cuts with ffmpeg scene detection, and confirm each one by looking at the frames on either side. Make contact sheets of the whole video at 10 to 30 frames per second and look at them.

Split the analysis between two fresh agents so neither one rushes. One measures motion, the other measures music. Merge their results into one timeline.

The motion agent tracks every move. For each one it records the first frame, the frame of peak speed, the frames to 90 percent of the move, any overshoot, and the easing it matches, as a Remotion easing where possible. It also records every transition (cut, wipe, morph, zoom, an object handing off to the next), every text entrance and exit, how long anything holds still, colors, sizes and positions. Then it writes a shot list.

The music agent finds the song and its tempo, builds a beat grid with every downbeat and backbeat as a frame, and lists the strongest hits. It also separates any sound effects the creator added on top of the song, because each of those needs a visual event in the new video.

From both, write the rules that make the reference feel the way it does, usually 8 to 12. Every rule carries a measured number, like "arrivals reach 90 percent in 5 to 7 frames with no bounce". Then write one beat table with every meaningful event, its frame, the beat it sits on and what moves.

Also list what in the reference you should not copy. Blank first frames make a bad cover. A blank ending wastes seconds. Text near the edges or the bottom of the frame gets covered by the platform's buttons. A landscape reference needs positions reworked for a vertical frame, not just cropped.

Show them the rules in plain English with stills, and ask which parts they love most and which they don't care about.

4. Interview them

Start with the easy questions, and cover these:

  1. What is it, in one sentence, and who is it for?
  2. Where will the video go? Default to vertical, 1080x1920, for reels and TikTok. Ask whether it should match the reference's length.
  3. What's the one thing someone should do after watching? Download, visit, book, buy or follow. Pick one.
  4. What do they already have? Logo, colors, fonts, screenshots, screen recordings, real customer quotes. If it's an app or a site, ask for a short screen recording of it in use, and give them the steps.
  5. What's their thing's version of each part of the reference? If the reference has a mascot, do they have one, or could they? If it shows a phone, what device fits theirs? If it shows a number, what's their number?
  6. Is there anything that must not appear, like real names, faces, private messages or prices that might change?

Then read back a short summary in plain language and ask whether you got it right.

5. Write the storyboard

Go through the reference beat by beat, on its own timeline, and write what happens in theirs at each moment. The frame stays. What's on screen changes. Every character, object, word and color in the reference gets a counterpart from their thing.

  • Map the palette. Swap the reference's colors for theirs role by role, so the main field color becomes theirs, the accent becomes theirs, and so on.
  • Write their own words. Give two or three options for the hook and any joke, and let them choose. Never reuse the reference's wording.
  • Rework positions for their format and keep every word inside the safe zone. On vertical video, keep key text away from the right edge and out of the bottom fifth of the frame.
  • When a move from the reference would break a rule, like a giant fly-in cropped by the edge of the frame, either soften it or name it as an exception and ask them.
  • End with a "kept and changed" list. Kept covers timing, motion and beat order. Changed covers everything you can see or read.
  • List the decisions only they can make, like colors, fonts, exact copy and where any emphasis color can appear.

Before any animation, render two or three style frames of key moments and get a pick.

Write PLAN.md and keep it to a page or two. It needs the format, the palette and typefaces, the storyboard, the kept and changed list, their decisions, what's left out so it doesn't creep back in, and what "done" means as a list they can check by watching. Walk them through it and get a yes before you build.

6. Build in passes, with a check-in after each

Put every event frame in one timeline file, and have every scene read its timing from there. Compute beat frames from seconds (frame = time × fps) rather than counting whole frames per beat, because a whole-frame grid drifts a little more every beat.

Build the reference's measured easings once as named presets and reuse them everywhere, so the motion stays consistent.

For every pass:

  1. Build it.
  2. Render it and check it yourself first, using the checks in section 7.
  3. Tell them where the file is and the one or two things to judge. Ask them to watch it on their phone, once with sound and once on mute.
  4. Ask them directly whether it's right, and only move on after a clear yes.
  5. Commit, then update PLAN.md.

A good order:

  1. A rough cut. Plain shapes and their text on the beat grid, so they can judge the pace first.
  2. The hook, fully designed.
  3. Every other section in the chosen look.
  4. Sound and the final mix.
  5. The ending and the cover frame.

If the timing depends on the reference's song, make two files. A review preview carries the reference audio so they can judge the sync, and it is never posted. The upload master carries no borrowed audio. Tell them how to add the sound when they post (section 10).

7. Check your own work

Run these on every version before you show it to them.

  • Sync. Detect the events in your render and compare them to the plan. Every event should land within one frame of its beat. Report the worst one.
  • Side by side. Make a sheet of the reference and theirs at the same 20 to 30 moments. The motion should match and nothing you can see should.
  • A still can't show motion. Make a contact sheet of the whole video at 2 to 4 frames per second, and a strip of every frame around each transition. Look at them before you call anything done.
  • Shrink the frames to phone width, about 390 pixels, and check that every word still reads.
  • Every piece of text stays on screen long enough to read at least once. Short words need about half a second fully visible.
  • Nothing covers text that's still being read. Outgoing text shrinks or fades before the next thing lands on top of it.
  • Nothing vanishes mid-screen. Things leave the frame, shrink away or fade out.
  • Center text on its visible letters, not on its text box. Measure it.
  • The first frame works as a cover, with the hook already drawn. The last frame isn't blank.
  • Check every frame for private information and for anything from the reference.

8. Get a second opinion

After each full version, start a fresh agent to review it. It must not see your reasoning. Give it the render, the reference, PLAN.md and REFERENCE.md. Ask it to extract the frames itself, score the video out of 10 against the reference, list must-fix problems with the exact moment and the reason for each, and name the one change that would most improve it. Also ask it directly whether any moment looks copied rather than learned from.

Treat the review as claims to check, not orders. Verify each must-fix in the frames, fix the real ones, and tell them in plain English what changed and what you skipped and why. Stop when they're happy, not when the score reaches a number.

Rules that will save you time

Each of these cost real time on a video built this way.

  • Copying the reference's timing exactly can put a word underneath an object that's still moving. When that happens, move the text, keep the beat, and log the change in PLAN.md.
  • Remotion's CameraMotionBlur samples forward in time, which makes beat-locked moves look about a frame early. For anything locked to a beat, write a blur that samples around the frame instead.
  • Scaling a reference's big moves down to fit the safe zone is normal. A text block that grows 2x in landscape may need to stop at 1.5x in vertical. Say so in the plan instead of letting text run off the edge.
  • The reference's ending is often the weakest part. Rework it for the action you want them to take, instead of copying a fade to white.
  • When they say something feels wrong at a moment, make a frame strip of that second in both videos before you change anything. What they describe and what's on screen are often different problems.
  • If the same problem survives three attempts, something is built wrong underneath. Stop tuning, tell them what you think is going on and fix the structure.
  • Never say something works until you've checked it, and say how you checked.

Keep a memory for the next chat

Chats run out of room, and they'll start new ones often. Update these files after every pass, because anything not written down is lost when a chat ends.

  • PLAN.md holds the format, storyboard, decisions and done list, with what's finished and what's next.
  • REFERENCE.md holds the source, the credit, the measured rules and the beat grid.
  • LESSONS.md is a short list of mistakes that cost time, so the next chat doesn't repeat them.
  • BUILD-NOTES.md says how to rebuild the video and where to change the timing, words and colors.
  • CREDITS.md lists every photo, font, sound and clip in their video, its source and its license.

When a chat is getting long, tell them, and give them a short prompt to paste into a new chat in the same folder.

9. Deliver

Render the upload master as H.264 MP4 at the reference's frame rate with AAC audio, at the size from the plan. Export a cover image from the first frame or a frame they choose. If you used the reference's audio for review, render the preview too and label it clearly as not for posting.

10. Credit and post

Write a credit line for their caption that names and tags the creator from REFERENCE.md and says what they learned from it, like "Timing and motion learned from @creator's ad". Then tell them, in plain English:

  • Where the master, preview and cover are.
  • How to add the sound. If the reference's audio is a popular song, they shouldn't put it in the file. On Instagram they can open the original reel, tap its audio and use it, so the sound credits the creator. Otherwise they pick a track from the app's own library. Explain that the edit was timed to the reference's audio, so a different track won't line up.
  • What's in CREDITS.md.
  • Anything you know is still weak.
