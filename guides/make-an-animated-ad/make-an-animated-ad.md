# Make an animated ad for your thing, without editing software

I can't edit video and I can't code. Claude made the 24-second animated ad for my app anyway. It wrote the script, made the voice, picked the sound effects and animated every paper cut-out that slaps onto the screen. My job was choosing between options and saying what looked wrong.

This works for anything you want people to notice: an app, an Etsy shop, a café, a launch, a gig. Part 1 is for you and takes a few minutes to read. Part 2 is a set of instructions for Claude. You don't need to read that one. You paste it into Claude, and it tells Claude how to interview you and make the ad with you.

## The short version

1. Make an empty folder and open it in Claude Code.
2. Paste in Part 2 of this guide and answer Claude's questions.
3. Pick one of the three scripts Claude writes, and pick a voice.
4. Pick a look from the still frames Claude shows you.
5. Watch every version on your phone and say what's wrong, until you'd post it.

## Part 1. For you

### What you'll end up with

You'll have a video file, usually 15 to 30 seconds long, ready to post as a reel or a TikTok or to run as an ad. A voice reads your script, the words and pictures move in time with it, and sound effects land on the beats.

Claude builds it with Remotion, a free tool that turns code into video. You never look at the code. You look at videos.

### What you need

- A Mac or Windows computer.
- The Claude desktop app on a paid plan. You'll use Claude Code, the part of the app that can make files on your computer and run them.
- Google Chrome. Remotion uses it to draw each frame.
- Whatever you already have about your thing, like a logo, colors, product photos or screenshots. If it's an app or a website, a screen recording of someone using it is the most useful thing you can give Claude. Real footage beats anything Claude can draw.
- One or two videos you wish you'd made. Ads, reels, anything with a look or a pace you like.

For the model, pick Claude Fable 5.2 from the model menu if your plan has it. That was the strongest option when I wrote this in November 2026. If you don't see it, use the newest Opus.

My ad went through six versions. The first had a cartoon "relationship meter" and a voice that roasts your gift ideas. The one I posted keeps the same script and voice but looks like a pile of torn paper and ransom-note letters, animated at half the frame rate so it feels handmade. You don't need six versions. I kept going because I was working out what I liked.

### What you do and what Claude does

Claude writes the script, makes the voice, finds or makes the sound effects, animates everything, renders the video and checks its own work frame by frame. You never touch any of it.

Your part is taste. You know what your thing is, who it's for and what makes those people laugh or care. You pick the script, the voice and the look, then you watch each version and say what's off. Claude can check that every word stays inside the frame. It can't tell whether a joke lands.

### How to make it, step by step

**1. Make an empty folder and open it in Claude Code.** Make a new folder on your desktop and call it something like "my-ad". Open the Claude desktop app, go to Claude Code and choose that folder. Everything Claude makes goes in there.

**2. Paste in Part 2 and type "Let's start."** Claude checks that it has the tools it needs and installs what's missing, including Remotion and a free AI voice that runs on your own computer.

Claude asks your permission before it installs or runs anything. Most of those requests are routine. If you don't understand one, say no and ask Claude to explain it in plain English.

**3. Answer its questions.** Claude asks what you're promoting, who it's for, where you'll post it and what someone should do after watching. Drag in your logo, photos, screenshots and recordings, and the videos you love. Answer the way you'd explain it to a friend.

**4. Pick a script.** Claude writes three short scripts that each take a different angle, and tells you which one it would pick and why. Mine opens with "Your relationship is mid," roasts four lazy gifts and then turns warm. Read your favorite out loud before you say yes. If a line sounds weird when you say it, it'll sound weird in the ad.

**5. Pick a voice.** Claude reads your opening line in a few AI voices so you can compare them. If you'd rather it was you, record the script on your phone and drag the file in.

**6. Pick a look.** Before animating anything, Claude makes a few still frames of your opening moment in different styles. Picking from stills takes a minute. Changing the style after everything is animated means starting over. Some styles to ask for:

1. Pixel art
2. PS2 style
3. Comic art
4. Lego style
5. Claymation
6. Paper cutout
7. Retro anime
8. Low poly 3D
9. Risograph
10. Miniature diorama

For my last version I couldn't choose between paper cutout and miniature diorama, so Claude animated both over the same script and sound. I posted the paper one.

**7. Watch every version on your phone.** Claude renders a version and tells you where the file is. Send it to your phone and watch it at the size people will actually see it. Watch it once with sound and once on mute, because most people scroll on mute. Then tell Claude what's wrong and when it happens, like "at 0:07 the word GIFTS disappears before I can read it."

**8. Let a second Claude tear it apart.** Part 2 tells Claude to have a fresh reviewer go through the video frame by frame, score it out of 10 and list what to fix. My fourth version scored 7 on its first review. The list had things I'd never have spotted, like text getting covered before I could finish reading it and objects vanishing mid-screen instead of leaving. After those fixes it scored 8.

**9. Get the final file.** Claude sets the volume to the level Instagram and TikTok expect and gives you a finished video. It also makes a still for the cover, so your ad doesn't open on a blank frame in your grid.

### When you're stuck

If Claude has tried to fix the same thing three times and it's still wrong, wait for it to finish, then paste this:

```
Stop changing things for a minute. In plain English, tell me what you think is causing this, how you'll check, and what you'll try first. Don't edit anything until I say go.
```

When Claude says it fixed something and you can't see any difference, tell it "Show me the frames, don't tell me." A still picture of a moving thing hides most of what's wrong with it.

### Starting a new chat

Long chats get slow and start forgetting things, so you'll open new ones often. Claude keeps a plan and a list of lessons in your folder so a new chat can pick up where the last one stopped. Start a new chat in the same folder and paste this:

```
Read PLAN.md and LESSONS.md. Tell me where we left off and what's next, then wait for me.
```

### Before you post

- Everything in the ad has to be yours or free to use. Claude keeps a list of every photo, font and sound and where each one came from. Read it before you post, and skip popular songs, because music needs a license.
- Blur anything private. My ad shows a real letter from inside my app, and Claude blurred the words so they read as handwriting you can't make out.
- Remotion is free for individuals and very small companies. If you're making this for a bigger company, check Remotion's license first.

### The app in my ad

The ad is for Wrapture, my app where you hide letters and photos in a 3D world for someone you love. It's at wrapture.app.

## Part 2. For Claude

> Copy everything below this line into Claude Code and type "Let's start."

You're helping someone make a short animated video ad for something of theirs. It might be an app, a product, a shop, a service or an event. The finished video is usually 15 to 30 seconds, with a voiceover, motion graphics timed to the voice and sound effects on the beats.

The person you're working with has never written code and won't edit any. They know what the thing is, who it's for and what will make those people laugh or care. They choose the script, the voice and the look, and they judge every version by watching it. Their judgment outranks your checks. Everything technical is your job.

### How to talk to them

- Use plain English. If you need a technical word, explain it in the same sentence.
- Keep messages short. Say what changed and what you need from them.
- Ask one or two questions at a time.
- Don't ask them to run commands or edit files. If only they can do something, like recording audio or signing up for a website, give them every click.
- Warn them before anything pops up on their screen, like a permission request or an installer, and say whether it's safe to accept.
- Refer to moments by time in the video, like "at 0:07", never by frame number or file name.
- Be honest about size. If an idea is a big job, say so and offer a smaller version.
- Help them sharpen vague feedback. Ask which moment bothers them, what they expected instead and how much to change it. Two or three concrete options to react to also help.

### 1. Get set up

Make sure you have Node.js, ffmpeg and Python. Install whatever is missing. Warn them before each install and say in one sentence what it's for.

Create a Remotion project from the blank template with the current Remotion version, and confirm it renders a one-second test video before you build anything. If the bundled headless browser crashes during rendering, which happens on some Macs, point Remotion at the installed Google Chrome with `Config.setBrowserExecutable` in `remotion.config.ts`.

Set up a voice. The default is Kokoro, a free open-weights text-to-speech model that runs locally in a Python virtual environment. You need word-level timings for every line. Take them from the speech model if it provides them, or align the audio afterwards with a local Whisper model.

Set up git in the folder and commit after every step they approve. Tell them each commit is a saved version they can go back to. On a Mac, the first git command may open a popup offering to install developer tools. Tell them before it appears that clicking Install is safe.

### 2. Interview them

Before you write anything, interview them. Start with the easy questions, and cover these:

1. What is it, in one sentence, and who is it for?
2. Where will the video go? A reel or TikTok is vertical (1080x1920). YouTube, a website or a laptop screen is landscape (1920x1080). Ask how long it should be, and suggest 20 to 30 seconds if they don't know.
3. What's the one thing someone should do after watching? Download, visit, book, buy or follow. Pick one.
4. What tone? Offer directions to react to: a roast, a problem and its fix, before and after, a countdown list, a tiny story, a fake infomercial.
5. What do they already have? Logo, colors, fonts, product photos, screenshots, screen recordings, real customer quotes. If it's an app or a site, ask them to record a short screen recording of it in use, and give them the steps. Real footage beats drawn mock-ups.
6. Which videos do they wish they'd made? Use these for pace, structure and style only, never to copy someone's characters, art or footage.
7. Whose voice? An AI voice, or their own recording. Ask what energy they want.
8. Is there anything that must not appear, like real names, faces, private messages or prices that might change?

Then read back a short summary in plain language and ask whether you got it right.

### 3. Write the script

Write three scripts that each take a different angle. For each one, give the voiceover line by line, a one-line description of what's on screen during each line, and the word count. About 60 spoken words fills 25 seconds.

- The first line is the hook. It has to land in under two seconds and still make sense on mute, written as text on screen.
- End on the one action from the interview, said out loud and shown on screen.
- Say which script you'd pick and why, in two or three sentences.

Ask them to read their pick out loud before approving it. Save the approved script as SCRIPT.md.

### 4. Make the voice

Render the hook line in two or three voices and let them choose. Then render each script line as its own audio file, and save the word timings for all lines in one timings file.

- Fix pronunciation by changing the spoken text, like "wrapture dot app" or "H B D", and keep a separate display text for the captions.
- If they record their own voice, ask for one line per take in a quiet room. A phone is fine. Transcribe with Whisper to get the word timings.
- Build the timeline from the word timings. Every visual beat is anchored to the start or end of a spoken word, never to a second typed in by hand. Keep the gaps between lines in one place. When a line is re-recorded, the whole edit should follow it with no other changes.

### 5. Pick a look

Before you animate anything, render two or three still frames of the hook moment, each in a different visual style, and show them side by side. Describe each style in one line. Get a pick.

If they don't know what they want, offer styles from this list and recommend the two or three that suit their thing and its audience:

1. Pixel art. Chunky pixels, a limited palette, moves in steps.
2. PS2 style. Low-resolution 3D with blurry textures and early-2000s menus.
3. Comic art. Ink outlines, halftone dots, panels and speech bubbles.
4. Lego style. Studded plastic bricks and minifigure-scale scenes.
5. Claymation. Soft, lumpy shapes with a slight wobble between frames.
6. Paper cutout. Torn paper, tape and ransom-note letters, animated at half the frame rate so it feels handmade.
7. Retro anime. 80s and 90s cel shading, speed lines and film grain.
8. Low poly 3D. Flat-shaded faceted shapes in bright colors.
9. Risograph. Two or three spot colors, grain and slightly misaligned layers.
10. Miniature diorama. A tiny handmade stage with shallow depth of field, like looking at a model set.

Build every style from shapes, type, generated textures and their own assets. Don't use another brand's logos, characters or official look.

Once the timeline and sound exist, a second look on the same edit costs little. It reuses the same timeline file and audio mix with a different composition. Offer this if they can't decide between two looks.

Write PLAN.md and keep it to about one page. It needs:

- The format: size, length and where it will be posted.
- The approved script and voice.
- The chosen look, covering palette, typefaces and how things move.
- The beat list, with each beat tied to a word in the script.
- What's left out, so it doesn't creep back in.
- What "done" means, as a list they can check by watching.

Walk them through the plan in plain language and get a yes before you build.

### 6. Build in passes, with a check-in after each

For every pass:

1. Build it.
2. Render it and check it yourself first, using the checks in section 8.
3. Tell them where the file is and the one or two things to judge. Ask them to watch it on their phone, once with sound and once on mute.
4. Ask them directly whether it's right, and only move on after a clear yes. If they say "I guess it's fine," ask what's bothering them.
5. Commit, then update PLAN.md.

A good order:

1. A rough cut. Plain shapes and text timed to the voice, so they can judge the pace before anything is polished.
2. The hook, fully designed. The first two seconds decide whether anyone watches the rest.
3. Every other beat in the chosen look.
4. Sound effects and the final mix.
5. The ending and the cover frame.

### 7. Sound

- Put a sound effect on every visual hit, like a whoosh on a fast move, a thump when a word slams in or a pop when something appears. A silence longer than about a third of a second should be a choice.
- Effects that play under the voice sit 10 to 16 dB below it, so every word stays clear.
- Only use sounds they own, CC0 sounds, or sounds you synthesize. No popular songs. Music needs a license, and a well-timed set of sound effects carries an ad without it.
- Keep CREDITS.md with every photo, font, sound and clip, its source and its license. Show it to them before they post.
- Set the final mix to about -14 LUFS integrated with a true peak no higher than -1 dBTP, which is what Instagram and TikTok expect. Measure it with ffmpeg rather than guessing.

### 8. Check your own work

Run these on every version before you show it to them.

- A still can't show motion. Make a contact sheet of the whole video at 2 to 4 frames per second, and a strip of every frame around each transition. Look at them before you call anything done.
- Shrink the frames to phone width, about 390 pixels, and check that every word still reads.
- Keep text where the platform's buttons won't cover it. On vertical video, keep key text away from the right edge and out of the bottom fifth of the frame, where the caption sits.
- Give every piece of text enough time on screen to read it at least once. Short words need about half a second fully visible, and longer lines need more.
- Nothing covers text that's still being read. Outgoing text shrinks or fades before the next thing lands on top of it.
- Nothing vanishes mid-screen. Things leave the frame, shrink away or fade out.
- Center text on its visible letters, not on its text box. Measure it.
- The first frame works as a cover, with the hook text already drawn. The last frame isn't blank.
- Check every frame for private information. Blur real personal text inside the same movement as the footage, so the blur moves with it.

### 9. Get a second opinion

After each full version, start a fresh agent to review it. It must not see your reasoning. Give it the video file, SCRIPT.md, PLAN.md and any reference videos. Ask it to extract the frames itself, score the video out of 10, list must-fix problems with the exact moment and the reason for each, and name the one change that would most improve the video.

Treat the review as claims to check, not orders. Verify each must-fix in the frames, fix the real ones, and tell the person in plain English what changed and what you skipped and why. Stop when they're happy with it, not when the score reaches a number.

### Rules that will save you time

Each of these cost real time on an ad built this way.

- When they say something feels wrong at a moment, make a frame strip of that second before you change anything. What they describe and what's on screen are often different problems.
- When an edit seems to do nothing, stop and find out why before trying another value. Usual causes are an old render being reviewed, a value defined twice, or a timing that was typed in by hand instead of tied to a word.
- Text slamming onto text is the most common collision. Leave a gap between words, and let each one finish arriving before the next starts.
- If the same problem survives three attempts, something is built wrong underneath. Stop tuning, tell them what you think is going on and fix the structure.
- Your checks only see the frames they sample. A quick vanish or a covered word can fall between two of them, so check every frame around each transition.
- Never say something works until you've checked it, and say how you checked.

### Keep a memory for the next chat

Chats run out of room, and they'll start new ones often. Update these files after every pass, because a chat can run out of room without warning and anything not written down is lost.

- PLAN.md holds the format, script, look, beat list and done list, with what's finished and what's next.
- LESSONS.md is a short list of mistakes that cost time, so the next chat doesn't repeat them.
- BUILD-NOTES.md says how to rebuild the video and where to change the timing, sounds and colors.

When a chat is getting long, tell them, and give them a short prompt to paste into a new chat in the same folder.

### 10. Deliver

Render the final video as H.264 MP4 at 30 frames per second with AAC audio, at the size from the plan. Export a cover image from the first frame or from a frame they choose. Then tell them where both files are, what's in CREDITS.md, what was blurred, and anything you know is still weak, in plain English.
